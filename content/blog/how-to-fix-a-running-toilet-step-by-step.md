## The short answer

A running toilet is almost always caused by one of three things: a worn-out flapper that no longer seals, a fill valve that is set too high, or a fill valve that is failing internally. All three are cheap fixes — the parts cost $5 to $20 and the repair takes about 15 minutes. A toilet that runs constantly can waste 200 gallons of water per day, which means it is worth fixing the same day you notice it.

## Why toilets run

The mechanics are simple. When you flush, the handle lifts a chain that opens the flapper at the bottom of the tank. Water drains into the bowl, and the float drops. That tells the fill valve to let water in. When the tank fills back up, the float rises and the fill valve shuts off.

A running toilet means one of those steps is broken. Either water is leaking past the flapper into the bowl, or the fill valve is not shutting off cleanly. Both are easy to diagnose and fix.

## What you need before you start

- A replacement flapper (universal fit, about $5–$8)
- A replacement fill valve if needed (universal Fluidmaster or Korky, about $12–$20)
- An adjustable wrench or pliers
- A flathead screwdriver
- A bucket and old towel
- White vinegar for cleaning mineral deposits

**No special skills required.** If you can turn a screwdriver and follow instructions, you can fix a running toilet.

## Diagnose the problem in 30 seconds

Open the tank lid and watch what happens after you flush.

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Water trickles into the bowl constantly | Worn flapper — replace it |
| Water overflows into the overflow tube | Fill valve set too high — adjust it |
| Water runs, stops, then runs again randomly | Fill valve failing — replace it |
| Toilet runs after flush but stops if you jiggle the handle | Chain too tight or flapper misaligned |
| Loud hissing or screeching when tank fills | Fill valve debris or worn washer |

## Fix 1: Replace the flapper (most common)

If water is trickling into the bowl and you can hear it refill every few minutes, the flapper is almost certainly the problem. Over time, the rubber gets stiff, warped, or coated with mineral deposits and no longer seats tightly against the flush valve.

1. **Turn off the water.** The shutoff valve is behind the toilet, usually near the floor. Turn it clockwise until it stops.
2. **Flush the toilet** to drain the tank. Sponge out any remaining water at the bottom.
3. **Disconnect the old flapper.** Unhook the rubber ears from the pegs on the overflow tube. Unclip the chain from the flush lever arm.
4. **Compare the old flapper to the new one.** Most universal flappers fit almost any toilet, but check that the shape and size are close. If your toilet is an older model with a larger flush valve opening, you may need a jumbo flapper.
5. **Attach the new flapper.** Hook the rubber ears onto the pegs on the overflow tube. Clip the chain to the flush lever arm, leaving about 1/4 inch of slack — not too tight, not too loose.
6. **Turn the water back on** and test. Flush and watch the flapper seat properly. If it still leaks, the flush valve seat (the round opening at the bottom of the tank) may be rough or corroded.

**Common mistake:** Leaving the chain too tight. If the chain has no slack, the flapper cannot fully seat and water will leak past it constantly. Leave a small amount of slack — about the width of your thumb.

**Pro tip:** If the flush valve seat is rough, use a flush valve seat repair tool or a pumice stone to smooth it. You can also buy a flapper with a silicone seal that conforms better to imperfect surfaces.

## Fix 2: Adjust the fill valve water level

If water is running into the overflow tube (the tall pipe in the center of the tank), the water level is set too high. The fill valve is letting in more water than the tank can hold, so the excess spills down the overflow tube and into the bowl.

1. **Locate the adjustment mechanism** on the fill valve. On Fluidmaster models, there is a twist ring or a clip on the float cup. On older ball-float models, there is a screw on the fill valve body.
2. **Lower the water level.** For Fluidmaster models, pinch the clip and slide the float cup down. For ball-float models, turn the adjustment screw counterclockwise. The goal is to set the water level about 1/2 inch below the top of the overflow tube.
3. **Flush and check.** The water should stop filling when it reaches about 1/2 inch below the overflow tube. If it still overflows, lower the float a bit more.
4. **Check for a cracked overflow tube.** If the water level is correct but water still runs, the overflow tube itself may have a crack. This is rare but happens in older toilets. Replace the flush valve assembly if needed.

**Common mistake:** Setting the water level too low. If the water level is more than an inch below the overflow tube, the toilet may not flush with enough force to clear the bowl. Find the sweet spot — enough water for a good flush, but not so much that it overflows.

## Fix 3: Replace the fill valve

If the toilet runs intermittently — it stops, then starts again randomly — or if adjusting the water level does not stop the running, the fill valve is failing internally. The rubber diaphragm or washer inside has worn out and cannot shut off completely.

1. **Turn off the water and flush** to drain the tank.
2. **Disconnect the water supply line** from the bottom of the fill valve. Use a wrench, but do not overtighten — the nut is plastic on many models.
3. **Remove the lock nut** that holds the fill valve to the tank. The nut is on the outside of the tank, underneath where the supply line connects.
4. **Pull the old fill valve out** from inside the tank.
5. **Adjust the height of the new fill valve** to match your tank height. Most universal valves have a twist-lock adjustment. Set it so the top of the valve is about 1 inch above the top of the overflow tube.
6. **Install the new fill valve** through the hole in the tank. Tighten the lock nut from underneath — hand-tight plus a quarter turn with a wrench.
7. **Reconnect the water supply line.** Use a new rubber washer if the old one is crusty.
8. **Attach the refill tube** (the small rubber hose) to the top of the overflow tube. Clip it in place so it points down into the tube.
9. **Turn the water on** and check for leaks at the base of the fill valve. Adjust the water level as described in Fix 2.

**Pro tip:** Buy a universal fill valve that includes an adjustable float. Fluidmaster 400A and Korky 528 are the most common and fit almost any toilet. They cost about $15 and take 10 minutes to install.

## Fix 4: Quick fixes for minor issues

Not every running toilet needs a new part. Try these before buying anything:

- **Jiggle the handle.** If that stops the running, the chain is too tight or the flapper is slightly misaligned. Adjust the chain length.
- **Clean the flapper.** Mineral deposits can prevent a good seal. Wipe the flapper and the flush valve seat with white vinegar and a scrub pad.
- **Check the handle.** If the handle is loose or the flush lever arm is bent, the flapper may not lift properly. Tighten the handle nut or replace the flush lever assembly ($5).
- **Shorten the chain.** If the chain is too long, it can get caught under the flapper. Remove a few links with pliers.

## When to call a plumber

Stop and call a pro if:

- The toilet is leaking from the base (water on the floor around the toilet) — this is a wax ring problem, not a tank problem
- The tank is cracked — you need a new toilet
- The flush valve (the large opening at the bottom of the tank) is cracked or corroded — this requires removing the toilet to replace
- You have tried replacing both the flapper and the fill valve and the toilet still runs
- The shutoff valve is stuck or broken and you cannot turn the water off

A plumber typically charges $100 to $250 to diagnose and fix a running toilet, including the service call.

## How to prevent future problems

- **Replace the flapper every two years** as preventive maintenance. Rubber degrades even if the toilet is not used often.
- **Clean mineral deposits** from the tank every spring. Pour a cup of white vinegar into the overflow tube and let it sit for an hour, then flush.
- **Do not use drop-in toilet tank tablets** that contain bleach. They degrade rubber parts faster and can void your warranty. Use a tank clip-on cleaner instead, or just clean manually.
- **Listen for running water** once a month. Walk through your house at night when it is quiet and listen for toilets that cycle on their own. Catching a running toilet early saves water and money.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my toilet keep running after I flush?

The most common cause is a worn flapper that no longer seals the flush valve. Water leaks past it into the bowl, and the fill valve keeps running to replace it. Replace the flapper — it costs about $5 and takes five minutes.

### How do I replace a toilet flapper?

Turn off the water, flush to drain the tank, unhook the old flapper from the overflow tube pegs, attach the new one, and reconnect the chain with about 1/4 inch of slack. Turn the water back on and test.

### How do I adjust the water level in my toilet tank?

Locate the adjustment mechanism on the fill valve. On Fluidmaster models, pinch the clip and slide the float cup down to lower the water level. The water should stop about 1/2 inch below the top of the overflow tube.

### What causes a toilet to run intermittently?

An intermittent running toilet — one that runs, stops, then runs again — is usually a failing fill valve. The internal diaphragm or washer is worn and cannot shut off cleanly. Replace the fill valve.

### When should I replace the entire fill valve instead of just the flapper?

Replace the fill valve if adjusting the water level does not stop the running, if the toilet runs intermittently, or if the fill valve is more than 10 years old. A new universal fill valve costs about $15 and takes 10 minutes to install.
