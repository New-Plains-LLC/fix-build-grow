A sprinkler head that snapped off at ground level or sprays a geyser straight up is one of the cheapest outdoor repairs in the whole house. Most pop-up heads unscrew from a threaded fitting, and replacements cost less than a pizza. The work is simple — the only real risks are digging into your own irrigation line and installing a head that matches the wrong spray pattern.

## First, Figure Out What Actually Broke

There are three common failures, and the fix differs for each:

1. **Head snapped off at the base.** The plastic body cracked where it threads into the fitting — usually from a mower deck or a car tire. You'll see the stub still threaded into the ground.
2. **Head won't retract and sprays wildly.** Dirt or debris in the retraction spring, or a cracked nozzle. The head still works, just badly.
3. **Water seeps constantly after the zone shuts off.** Low-head drainage — water in the lateral lines emptying through the lowest head — not a broken head at all.

If the head is intact but leaking only after shutdown, skip straight to the callout below. Everything else in this guide applies to failures 1 and 2.

## What You Need

- Replacement pop-up sprinkler head (match brand family and spray pattern — see below)
- Flathead screwdriver or a sprinkler head removal tool
- Shovel or hand trowel
- Pipe thread sealant tape (Teflon tape)
- PVC riser, a swing joint, or a flexible funny-pipe fitting (only if the riser snapped too)
- A towel and a bucket of water for rinsing debris out of the hole

## Step 1: Shut Off the Water and Run the Zone Dry

Turn the irrigation controller to OFF, then close the shut-off valve for the sprinkler system. Now run the broken zone briefly at the controller — many systems drain the lateral lines when you do this. Less water in the trench means a cleaner repair and less mud.

## Step 2: Dig It Out — Carefully

Clear grass and soil back about 6 inches around the head, down to where the riser meets the fitting. Keep the shovel blade vertical and stay 2 inches clear of the head on all sides: your lateral supply line runs into that fitting, and one careless stab can turn a $10 fix into a $100 plumbing job.

Set the sod aside on a tarp in one intact piece — you'll reuse it as a patch.

## Step 3: Unscrew and Match the Head

Unscrew the broken head from the fitting by hand or with the removal tool. If the threaded riser snapped off inside the fitting, dig to expose the fitting and unscrew the whole assembly instead.

Now, before you buy anything, read the old head:

- **Spray pattern** stamped or molded on the nozzle: 8', 10', 12', 15' radius; full circle, half, quarter, or adjustable strip.
- **Brand family:** Rain Bird, Hunter, and Toro nozzles are not always interchangeable. Match the brand if you can.

Getting this wrong is the #1 mistake. A mismatched radius means a dry strip near the sidewalk or water blasting the house siding.

{{< callout >}}
**Bought a different brand or pattern anyway?** Most pop-up heads have interchangeable nozzle trees — pop the nozzle off the old head and clip it onto the new body, then set the radius screw. That gets you the correct spray pattern even when the store only had the generic body in stock.
{{< /callout >}}

## Step 4: Wrap, Install, and Align

Wrap the fitting threads (not the head threads that screw onto them) with 2-3 wraps of Teflon tape, threaded clockwise. Screw the new head on hand-tight plus a quarter turn — don't gorilla it; over-tightening cracks the fitting.

Before backfilling, orient the nozzle so the spray pattern squares up to the lawn edges. Some heads thread-rotate to adjust; others have a clutch ring you hold while turning the body.

## Step 5: Flush, Test, and Backfill

Turn the water on and run the zone for 30 seconds with the head exposed:

1. **Watch for spray alignment** — adjust the radius screw or pattern as needed.
2. **Check the fitting for leaks** — drips at the base mean the Teflon seal failed; unscrew, re-tape, and retry.
3. **Confirm retraction** — after the zone shuts off, the head should sink flush. If it stays up, pull the stem up, rinse the spring channel with clean water, and work it a few times.

Once it all checks out, backfill with the native soil, tamp lightly, and press the saved sod back over. Water it for a week so the patch knits in.

{{< warning >}}
**Before you dig more than a few inches, call 811 for a free utility locate.** Sprinkler lateral lines are shallow, but electrical low-voltage wire to valves, gas service lines, and cable conduits can run through the same yard. Hand-dig near any marked utility. Also: never stand directly over a sprinkler head when testing a zone — a head that's failed under pressure can launch the nozzle or stem at face height.
{{< /warning >}}

## When To Call a Pro

- **Multiple heads failed in the same zone** — that pattern points to a pressure problem or a cracked lateral line, not bad luck.
- **Water pools between the water meter and the backflow preventer** even with the system off — that's the main line, and it's a licensed-plumber job.
- **The fitting itself is broken underground** and you can't get a clean grip on it — an irrigation tech can dig, cut, and re-fit in under an hour.
- **You're on a shared or HOA-maintained system** — many associations require their contractor for any changes, and non-standard heads can throw off the whole zone's coverage.

A repair that's beyond a head swap usually runs $75-$150 per visit from an irrigation service — fair money when the alternative is a flooded trench or a ruined zone.

## Common Mistakes

- **Installing the wrong spray radius.** Your lawn gets watered unevenly, and you won't notice until brown patches show up in August.
- **Overtightening into the fitting.** Hand-tight plus a quarter turn. Cracked fittings are underground repairs.
- **Skipping the flush test before backfilling.** Debris in the line from the break will clog the new nozzle immediately — flush first, then trust the spray.
- **Leaving the head too high or too low.** Set the top of the head about a half inch above soil grade in turf (lower in mulch beds) so mowers don't clip it and water still reaches the grass.
