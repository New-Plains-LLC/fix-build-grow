Walk across your deck barefoot in the fall, after a summer of sun and rain, and you'll feel them: boards that rock under your feet, screw heads sitting proud of the wood, nail points sticking up an inch. Popped fasteners are the single most common wood deck problem — and the most commonly ignored, right up until someone catches a toe and goes down hard.

The fix is simple, cheap, and takes an afternoon. Here's how to do it right, and how to tell when a board is too far gone to save.

{{< warning >}}
Standing on a compromised deck can be dangerous. If whole sections feel spongy, or boards are rotted through, stop and have a structural inspection before repairing surfaces. Surface repairs won't fix a failing frame.
{{< /warning >}}

## Why Deck Fasteners Pop in the First Place

Deck boards move. Every wet/dry cycle swells and shrinks the wood, and that constant motion works fasteners loose a little at a time. Three things make it worse:

- **Nails.** Smooth-shank nails have almost no grip once the wood moves. If your deck was framed with nails — common in older builds — expect recurring pop-ups.
- **Face-mounted screws without pilot holes.** Screws driven in without pilot holes split the board ends over time, and split wood loses its hold.
- **Missing joist connection.** A board popping in the same spot every year often means the fastener isn't hitting solid joist wood underneath.

{{< callout >}}
Walk the entire deck and mark every problem spot with painter's tape or chalk before touching a tool. Fixing as you find leads to missed spots and a second lap.
{{< /callout >}}

## What You'll Need

- Drill/driver and joist-deck screws (structural wood screws or coated #10 deck screws, 3 inch)
- Hammer and nail set (if the deck is nailed)
- Circular saw or oscillating multi-tool (only if boards need replacing)
- Replacement deck boards matched to your existing lumber
- Sandpaper or a sanding block (80 and 120 grit)
- Deck brightener or cleaner for splintered areas

Total cost runs $10–$60 depending on whether you're re-fastening only or swapping a few boards.

## Fix 1: Re-Secure Popped Screws

The amateur move is driving the same backed-out screw back in. It will pop out again — the hole is already stripped. Instead:

1. **Remove the old screw completely.** Back it out with the drill.
2. **Drive a new, slightly larger screw** at least 1 inch to the side, in fresh wood, centered on the joist below. Locate the joist by looking at neighboring boards' fastener lines or by sounding the board with a knuckle.
3. **Set the head flush,** barely below the surface. Overdriving crushes wood fibers and creates a new weak point.

If you must reuse the same spot, fill the stripped hole with a hardwood dowel and exterior glue, let it cure, then screw into the patch.

## Fix 2: Fix Popped Nails — Don't Just Hammer Them Back

Hammering a popped nail back in is a temporary fix at best; it will pop again on the next humidity swing. Two options:

- **Best: remove the nail** and replace it with a deck screw in the same hole (screws hold far better in nail holes than nails do).
- **Acceptable: pull the nail, offset a new screw an inch to the side,** and fill the old nail hole with exterior wood filler.

Never just drive a nail down and leave the point poking through the underside of the board where someone standing below can find it with their head.

## Fix 3: Repair Splintered and Cracked Boards

Popped fasteners and splinters travel together — once a board moves, the surface breaks apart. For minor splintering:

1. Sand the area with 80-grit until smooth, then 120-grit to finish.
2. Clean off dust and apply a deck brightener to even out the color.
3. Re-seal the sanded patch after it dries, since raw wood absorbs water fast.

For a split running the length of a board, driving screws at both ends of the crack slows it, but the board is on borrowed time. Plan a replacement.

## Fix 4: Replace a Board That's Beyond Saving

When a board is cracked through, rotted, or split past repair:

1. **Cut out the bad board** between joists with a circular saw (set depth to board thickness — do not cut into joists) or remove fasteners and pry it free.
2. **Inspect the joist top** underneath. Soft, dark, crumbling wood means rot spread further than the board — that's a bigger job.
3. **Cut and install a matching board.** Pressure-treated lumber should be installed bark-side up and allowed to weather 1–3 months before staining, or use pre-finished boards to match.
4. **Fasten with two screws per joist,** with pilot holes drilled to prevent end splitting.

{{< callout >}}
Match the gap between boards to your existing deck — usually 1/8 to 1/4 inch. Boards butted tight against each other trap water and rot faster than anything else on the list.
{{< /callout >}}

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Using interior screws.** They rust, streak the wood, and fail within a couple of seasons. Only use coated exterior or stainless deck screws.
- **Overdriving fasteners flush and below.** A screw buried deep holds less and invites splitting.
- **Replacing with mismatched lumber.** A bright new board in a weathered deck looks worse than the damage did. Weather it or stain to match.
- **Skipping the underside check.** Damage you see on top often has rot underneath. Flip a removed board over before throwing it away to see what your joists may be dealing with.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a deck contractor if:

- Multiple boards feel spongy or the frame moves when you lean on the railing
- Ledger board (where the deck attaches to the house) shows rot, rust, or gaps — a failing ledger is how decks collapse
- More than 20–30% of boards need replacement — a full re-deck usually costs less than piecemeal work
- The structure is more than 20 years old and has never been inspected

## Make It a Habit

Walk your deck twice a year — spring and fall. Ten minutes with a broom handle (drag it across; loose fasteners and popped boards rattle) catches problems while they're still an afternoon fix instead of a replacement project.
