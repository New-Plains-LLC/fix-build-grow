You wake up one morning and the double-pane window in your living room looks like it's permanently milky. You can't wipe it off from inside or outside — because the fog is *between* the panes. That cloudiness isn't a cleaning problem. It's a failed window seal, and it's one of the most common, most confusing, and most expensive-sounding home problems a homeowner will face.

The good news: you don't always need to rip out the whole window. Here's how to diagnose the issue, your three realistic options, and what each actually costs.

## First, Confirm It's a Failed Seal (Not Just Condensation)

Not every wet-looking window is a busted seal. Do this quick test before you spend a dime:

- **Wipe the glass.** If the moisture is on the *surface*, inside or outside, that's normal condensation — a humidity problem, not a seal problem.
- **Check the weather.** Fog that appears every morning and clears by afternoon, mainly on the inside, is interior condensation. Fog that's stuck between the panes *permanently* — day and night, sun or rain — is a failed seal.
- **Try the cold test.** On a cool morning, run your palm across both panes. Condensation *between* the glass can't be reached at all.

{{< callout >}}
What actually fails is the seal around the edges of the insulating glass unit (the IGU — the sealed pair of panes with a spacer between them). Once that edge seal leaks, moisture vapor gets in and condenses on the cold glass, leaving that foggy film you can't wipe away. This is why the haze is often worst at the bottom corners and creeps up over time.
{{< /callout >}}

If it's truly between the panes, you have three paths forward. They are not equal in cost, results, or effort.

## Why a Broken Seal Matters Beyond Looks

A foggy window isn't just ugly — it's a sign that your window has lost the gas and the thermal coating that made it energy-efficient. Modern double-pane units are filled with argon or krypton and have a low-E coating to reflect heat. When the seal fails, that gas leaks out and the window becomes, functionally, a single pane with a permanently dirty gap.

That means measurable heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer, higher energy bills, and condensation that can eventually rot a wood frame or wrinkle window treatments. Left unchecked, a failed unit is both a comfort problem and a slow money leak.

## Option 1: The DIY Defog — Low Cost, Temporary, Not for Everyone

There are defogging kits sold for failed-seal windows. The idea: drill small holes in the bottom of the glass, pump in a cleaning solution, swab the interior, then let it dry with a vent and plug the holes. A basic kit runs $15–$40 plus your time.

{{< warning >}}
Defogging does **not** restore the seal or the argon gas. It just clears the current fog. The seal is still broken, so moisture will likely return within months. It can also void a transferable window warranty and may leave drilling marks that a future buyer notices. Treat it as a short-term cosmetic fix, not a repair — and know that it won't fix heat loss.
{{< /warning >}}

If the window is old, cheap, or you're just trying to sell a property cosmetically, defogging might buy you breathing room. But set expectations honestly: it's a patch, not a cure.

## Option 2: Replace Only the Glass Unit — The Smart Middle Ground

For most homeowners, replacing just the insulating glass unit (the "sealed unit" or "IGU") is the right balance of cost and result. You keep your existing frame, sashes, and hardware — you swap only the glass package.

- **Cost:** Roughly $100–$300 per unit for the glass plus labor, or $60–$150 if you're handy and source the unit yourself. Far cheaper than a full window.
- **Result:** You restore the seal, the gas, the low-E coating, and the clear view — and you recover much of your energy performance.
- **Fit:** This only works if your frame and sashes are in good shape. It's also difficult for some fixed transoms or unusual sizes.

{{< callout >}}
Measure the glass package carefully — not the visible glass, but the full unit including the spacer that sits inside the frame. Better still, pop out the sash and measure the glass edge-to-edge. Ordering the exact IGU size from a local glass shop or online window-glass supplier is the difference between a clean install and a custom-cut headache.
{{< /callout >}}

## Option 3: Replace the Whole Window — When It's Truly Worth It

Full window replacement is the most expensive route, but it's right in specific situations:

- The frame, sill, or sash is rotted, warped, or beyond repair.
- The window itself is very old, inefficient single-pane that "upgraded" poorly, or out of warranty with failing hardware.
- You have several failing windows and want to improve the whole house's energy rating at once.
- You're changing window style, size, or operation anyway.

- **Cost:** $300–$850+ per window installed, depending on size, material (vinyl vs. wood vs. fiberglass), and whether it's a full-frame or insert (pocket) replacement.
- **Result:** A brand-new window with a fresh warranty and modern efficiency — and, done well, you can claim energy-efficiency rebates in many areas.

## Decide With One Question

Ask yourself: *Is the frame worth saving?*

- **Frame and sashes are solid, window is otherwise fine** → replace just the glass unit (Option 2).
- **Frame is old, rotting, or the window is already on its last legs** → replace the whole window (Option 3).
- **You just need it to look okay fast, on a rental or a quick flip, and you accept it won't last** → consider defogging (Option 1), but don't kid yourself about the outcome.

## When to Call a Pro

You can absolutely source and order the glass yourself, but a few situations call for a professional window or glass installer:

- The window is large, on a second story, or in a tight stairwell where handling heavy glass is risky.
- The frame shows significant rot, water damage, or structural flex — replacing glass onto a bad frame is wasted money.
- Your home is historic or uses non-standard panes that must be custom-made and glazed on site.
- Condensation has spread to the frame, sill, or surrounding wall, suggesting the moisture problem is bigger than one unit.

Before committing, stress-test your choice: if the frame is sound and you defog instead of replacing the unit, you've paid to hide a symptom and will likely pay again in months. If you replace the whole window when only the seal failed, you've spent hundreds more than the IGU route. The economical answer is almost always: save the good frame, replace the bad glass unit.

## The Bottom Line

A foggy double-pane window is not a cleaning problem and not always a full-replacement emergency. Diagnose it once: if the haze is trapped between the panes, you have a failed seal. Between the low-cost defog patch and the high-cost full replacement sits the sweet spot most homeowners want — replacing just the glass unit to restore the seal, the gas, and the view without ripping out a perfectly good frame. The single most important decision is whether your frame is worth saving. Answer that honestly, and the right path (and the right price) becomes clear.
